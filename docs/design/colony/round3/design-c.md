# Cinderhill

A storm has loosened a sheet of bark above your nest. Hundreds of ants are already streaming through the gap beneath it. Draw a trail and the front rank turns under your finger. The first seed comes home in nine seconds. Its kernel feeds the brood. Its husk is big enough to roof a stretch of trail or wedge open a second entrance. You have one husk. Rain starts in fifteen seconds. **Cinderhill** is a real-time strategy game about building a place where a great many small animals can live. You command traffic, excavate chambers, and fight for access to a changing patch of ground. The best moment comes when you lift your finger and a thousand ants carry out a plan you can recognize by its shape.

## The core loop

| Time | What the player does | What makes it satisfying |
|---|---|---|
| 30 seconds | Read a change in the field. Draw or redirect a trail. Watch the first carriers complete the trip. Spend their cargo somewhere visible. | The first ants turn immediately. By the time the tail of the column reaches the fork, the head has changed the map. |
| 10 minutes | Secure a food source, grow a cohort, and build two viable routes through a forecast event. Choose what the colony takes into the next clearing. | A piece of infrastructure earns its cost in front of you. A crowded tunnel empties, or a column emerges under cover on the far side of a contested verge. |
| 5 hours | Establish a colony across a branching watershed. Specialize its brood and its works. In the final chapter, move the queen, brood, and winter stores through a network you built and tested yourself. | Earlier choices survive in the landscape. You know which crossing you can lose, which entrance must stay open, and where the next generation will hatch. |

## The first five minutes

**The first order lands at 2 seconds, the first food at 9, the first birth at 14, the first material choice at 18, and the first threat reaches the trail at 30.** These are target times for the direct route on the fixed opening map. The clock below describes one successful playthrough. The rain forecast begins when the first husk reaches the fork. A player inspecting the scene gets the same decision window.

Start with 480 adults, a queen, four mature pupae, and an occupied nest. The screen opens close enough to see individual jaws. A single camera frame contains the seed, the entrance, and the fork. A narrow cutaway under the entrance shows the brood. Most ants already have work. Forty-eight available foragers are passing the seed. The player can change a running colony with one stroke.

The roof choice plays differently. The column keeps its short route through the shower and strips the first seed sooner. The second entrance remains shut. At the next grass head the roof player has food in hand and a fresh excavation decision. The wedge player has two entrances and fewer stores. Both answers carry forward. Without a placement, exposed carriers put loads down and retrieve them after drainage. The player can watch one entire shower, understand the cost, and recover.

**Minute 2.** The grass head holds more kernels than the entrance bin can take. Tap it to see the estimated trips and the time until rain. Draw a second trail to tender grubs under a curled leaf, or concentrate on finishing the grain. Grain sustains work. Protein commits the next brood. A visible clutch begins development when fed. The founding pupae make the first birth observable immediately. The new clutch establishes the actual growth rate.

**Minute 3.** A rival column appears from the far side of the grass head. Its destination and approximate strength are visible. Hold the seed with guards, take the unguarded grubs, or cut a passage from the second entrance to the rear of the grass. Each answer changes where the next fifty carriers travel. A rival that wins carries off actual kernels. The player can see exactly what the encounter cost.

**Minute 4.** The first player-fed cohort emerges if the player secured protein. Select its work by placing a work order. The new adults join it in a visible wave. The field now offers a heavy object, a curled leaf large enough to span the first wash. Assigning its crew thins the food column until the leaf is seated. The opening husk needed three ants. This lift needs thirty-two.

**Minute 5.** Pull back to see both entrances, the wash, the rival approach, and the next bank. The chapter objective appears on that bank as a cutaway silhouette of a nursery. Establish it, feed one clutch there, and bring it through high water. The player has already practiced every required action. The remaining problem is choosing a site and keeping it supplied.

## Commanding the colony

**Draw the route.** Touch a trail, entrance, or source and drag to a destination. The line follows the stroke, including intentional detours. Before release, a broken stripe marks an impassable segment. A tap on a source offers a valid suggested route for small screens and players who prefer taps. Touching an existing fork and dragging edits only the downstream segment. Loaded carriers finish their current trip if it remains traversable. Empty ants take the revised route at the next junction.

**Assign the effort.** Tap a destination to open a thumb-sized control with *Light*, *Steady*, and *Surge*. These set the destination’s share of available labor. The preview shows the workers it would draw and which current orders would lose them. A surge to finish a bridge before rain can leave food sitting uncollected. Commands attach to places. Players can follow one marked worker for pleasure without ever having to select it to get work done.

**Control time and view.** Drag bare ground to pan. Pinch to zoom. Tap the entrance to expand the nest cutaway in place. A permanently reachable hold button freezes solo play while the player inspects, draws, or revises queued orders. Resume applies them together. Mouse drag and click perform the same actions. Space holds time, Tab cycles active works, and Enter confirms. Shape, motion, and fill pattern distinguish jobs and hazards in both art looks. Every essential cue works with sound muted.

## Ground, food, brood, and force

| Decision | Rule and consequence |
|---|---|
| **Which loads deserve a trip?** | Kernels fill the work reserve. Protein feeds clutches and determines how many eggs can develop. A ripe source shows remaining loads. The entrance shows delivered loads per ten seconds and the longest unloading queue. Opening another food trail can reduce total deliveries if both return through the same door. |
| **Where should the nest grow?** | Excavate contiguous cells in a side cutaway. Digging needs labor. Spoil travels to a designated surface dump. A shallow nursery hatches quickly in warm ground and floods early. A deep nursery stays dry behind a sill but costs more trips to feed. Chambers need accessible floor and an open face for work. An extra door can be worth more than another room. |
| **Which adults should hatch?** | At feeding time, commit a clutch to workers or guards. Workers haul, dig, nurse, and lift. Guards hold frontage and escort loads. Guards eat from the same reserve and do little excavation. A fed worker clutch takes about 75 seconds, a guard clutch about 110. The player sees developing bodies and the next emergence time. Empty stores suspend new work commitments. Existing adults can still forage and recover. |
| **Where does a heavy object belong?** | Husks, stems, stones, and curled leaves exist as individual objects with authored placements. A stem spans water or braces a tunnel mouth. A leaf roofs a depot or ferries stores across a rising pool. Preview the required crew, travel path, and resulting clearance before lifting. A crew can reverse course. Once seated, moving the object requires another full lift. |
| **Where is a fight worth the labor?** | Draw a hold line across a trail or assign guards to an escort. Contact resolves by occupied frontage, guard strength, and access for relief. A narrow door lets a small guard force delay a larger one but also throttles friendly carriers. Losing ants yield ground and retreat. Dropped cargo stays contested. Recall frees the guards and concedes the site. There is no reward for pursuing a retreating column. |
| **Which ground can the colony afford to lose?** | Water follows authored elevation and outlets. The next crest has a visible height mark. Birds take exposed food and scatter foragers in the footprint of a passing shadow. Rivals seek profitable sources, then withdraw when hauling costs exceed their gain. A roof answers the bird. A raised route answers water. An empty decoy lane costs the rival time only while it still leads to food. |

Each clearing has a surface about two phone screens wide at working zoom and one connected nest beneath it. Entrances correspond across views. Rocks, slopes, and bark stay recognizable when the camera turns from Field daylight to Ember dusk. The player can inspect the ground from edge to edge before committing a crew. Weather and rival arrivals are seeded. Their approach is visible early enough to change an order.

## Campaign and session shape

The watershed contains six chapters and optional side clearings. Chapter one takes about 10–14 minutes on a first clear. Later chapters contain two or three 8–15 minute operations. The authored route through the campaign targets four to six hours including retries and optional works. Each chapter introduces one strategic constraint, then combines it with earlier ones. The sequence is runoff, limited frontage, split entrances, moving food, sustained high water, and migration.

The persistent unit is a **settlement**. Its entrances, chambers, seated works, brood allocation, and secured stores survive departure. At each chapter end choose what travels with the founding party. Extra guards can win the next headland. A leaf already rigged for hauling can establish a crossing before rivals arrive. Taking either ties up workers that could have carried food. Side clearings let the player acquire the asset they left behind at the cost of another operation.

An operation ends on a physical event. In chapter one, high water crests and recedes with a fed clutch alive in the new nursery. The camera follows the first new worker out of that room and onto the route that sustained it. A failed crest costs the exposed clutch. The player can recover with surviving adults or rewind to the forecast checkpoint. Save on each accepted order and periodically during quiet play. Closing the tab stops solo time. Tomorrow’s reason to return is the unresolved choice visible on the saved map, such as a completed crossing with two founding sites beyond it. There is no attendance reward.

**Replay acceptance targets**

- On the direct opening route, first visible response within two ticks of an accepted order, first delivery within 10 seconds of release, first emergence by 16 seconds of active play. Track input latency and delivery latency separately.
- Both husk placements must yield a successful first crest under reference play. Removing the chosen work in a replay fork must change delivered loads or reachable cells during the shower. A cosmetic choice fails this check.
- In the opening five minutes, no required construction or growth wait exceeds 20 seconds without another feasible order that changes the outcome. Verify feasibility from labor, access, resources, and remaining time.
- At minutes two and four, record route occupancy, delivery rate, adult count, and active crews. A bot that surges every source must unload fewer kernels on the congested test map than one that opens the second face.
- Measure unaided chapter completion from first-time human sessions. Target at least 8 of 10 in an initial usability round. Record time to first touch, first abandoned order, and where each player stopped. The replay can locate a stall. Only the player can tell us why they left.

## Friends by link

A solo player can invite up to three friends into the current clearing. The link opens directly in a browser. Guests claim a color and a cursor. Everyone commands the same colony. One player can route food, another excavate the nursery, and two can coordinate a guard withdrawal with a heavy lift. No role is mandatory. Touching a work marks it as being edited. Conflicting edits show the accepted route and let the second player redraw. Guests can ping a location with one tap. A shared hold request pauses when everyone agrees.

For competition, send a **same-seed challenge** from a completed operation. Friends receive the same starting snapshot and forecast. Rank first by surviving brood, then by delivered reserve at the crest. Show tied results as ties. After finishing, overlay a friend’s trails at the same tick or watch their replay. Nobody needs to be online together.

A Cloudflare Worker serves room entry and issues scoped guest links. A Durable Object sequences room commands, runs the authoritative 20 Hz simulation, and checkpoints the snapshot and command log. Clients run the same integer simulation and interpolate rendering. Reconnect from a snapshot plus accepted commands. Deduplicate retried commands by guest and sequence number. The founder keeps the owner capability separately from the invitation. Solo play runs locally and persists in IndexedDB. Creating a room copies the current checkpoint. A disconnected guest can rejoin without forking the shared colony.

## Three reasons to play Cinderhill

1. **The swarm makes the plan legible.** The 5,000-ant sandbox held the owner for seventeen minutes. That supports keeping a dense, freely drawable swarm at the center of play. It does not establish how many ants a stranger needs. Start at 480, then test the count. Judge the result by whether someone spots congestion, changes a fork, and watches the queue drain without opening a panel.
2. **One object supports competing permanent works.** The seed starts as food. Its empty husk becomes a consequential piece of construction. The player remembers choosing that particular door every time a column passes under it. Later, relocating a bridge while stores are still crossing it becomes an operation in its own right.
3. **A colony can win by giving up ground on time.** Forecasts, transport capacity, brood maturity, and retreat all use the same clock. The final migration rewards a player who closes an entrance early enough to move the last clutch. Surface territory, head count, and combat strength alone cannot settle that decision.

## Build order and release scope

1. **Prove the fork.** Reuse the measured integer simulation, flow fields, shaped trails, hashes, and replay recorder. Build the fixed opening through the first shower in plain ES modules. Render each roof, door, kernel, and pupa from simulation state. Compare roof, wedge, and no-order replays. Play it on physical phones.
2. **Finish chapter one.** Ship the whole nursery objective internally, including an idle start, bad placement, failed crest, rewind, save, and victory. Use procedural Field silhouettes and halftone ground in daylight. Use Ember rims, warm entrances, and restrained sparks at dusk. Give loads, feet, rain, and packed soil distinct procedural sounds.
3. **Prove sustained strategy.** Build split entrances and the migration finale before filling in the middle chapters. Require two materially different winning colony builds. Keep terrain authored and use bounded seed variation for food, rival timing, and placements. Preserve readable geometry across seeds.
4. **Prove shared play.** Add room links, late join, conflict handling, disconnect recovery, and same-seed challenges. Verify hashes through an actual four-browser session and server restart. Package native ES modules and procedural assets for direct web delivery.
5. **Release the campaign.** The first public release includes six chapters, free trail painting, solo saves, cooperative links, challenge replays, Field and Ember, touch and desktop controls, and a shareable chapter-one entry. Additional watersheds and competitive scenarios follow only after strangers finish and share the first one.

Cut optional side clearings, extra object types, replay overlays, and cosmetic variation first. Preserve the opening choice, readable hauling, complete campaign ending, and working friend link. Spend the first art budget, if approved, on improving silhouettes at phone scale.

## Five risks to test early

| Risk | Early test and decision |
|---|---|
| The opening feels scripted. | Give first-time players the fixed clearing with both placements live. Ask them to predict what will happen at the fork, then watch the shower. If both routes look equivalent, exaggerate their geometry and delivery difference before adding content. |
| The phone cannot show enough ants and still accept precise input. | Run the opening and a late 5,000-ant crossing on physical iOS and Android phones. Measure missed forks, frame stalls, heat, and input-to-turn latency. Reduce particles and distant animation first. Prior performance evidence covers emulated iPhone rendering on a Mac. |
| One route or build dominates. | Run roof and wedge openings, worker-heavy and guard-heavy broods, and shallow and deep nurseries across the same authored seeds. Inspect wins and transport costs. Change frontage, distances, or forecasts when one policy wins every comparison. |
| The colony becomes unreadable under pressure. | Freeze an unfamiliar replay just before a crest. Ask a player to point to the threatened clutch, its supply route, and one usable response. If they need a dashboard to answer, redraw the waterline, cargo, and entrances. |
| Hosting or reconnecting corrupts a good run. | Drop a guest during a heavy lift, duplicate the last command, restart the room, and rejoin after the crest. Compare final hashes and cargo ownership with uninterrupted play. A husk must have exactly one location and one crew assignment throughout recovery. |

## Two choices for the owner

1. **Which image should someone remember after chapter one?** Pick the camera treatment for the first successful crest. **Field** stays in daylight and tracks a single pale worker through the busy colony. **Ember** reaches dusk at the crest and reveals the occupied nursery by entrance light. **Field to Ember** follows the final kernel down in daylight and emerges with the new worker after dark. My choice is **Field to Ember**.
2. **What should the last migration feel like?** **A close escape** ends as the last clutch clears rising water. **A deliberate departure** lets the player choose the hour and watch the settlement empty. **A founding procession** puts the hardest crossing earlier and ends with the whole column arriving in open ground. My choice is **a deliberate departure**. It gives the campaign’s last order to the player.
