# Cinderhill’s first ten minutes

**Assumed post-mortem.** Players learned to draw a trail. They saw food come home, pupae open and ants shelter from a bird. They reached dusk. What too few could say was, “My hill looks like this because I chose to do that.” Some stopped at the chapter card. Others continued, lost brood in the first rain and left with a worse impression. My release blocker is the gap between **seeing the colony respond** and **making a decision worth repeating**. The spec measures the first much more reliably than the second. [02 §3.1, “Pace budget,” and §4, “The first five minutes”][spec] · [04, “Milestones,” R1 and Depth check][roadmap]

**Checked evidence.** The owner called the previous phone build “too slow and still too basic.” Across seven colony sessions, the owner could draw a trail within 2 to 3 seconds, yet food took 73 seconds after the trail or never arrived. No session showed a birth or a threat. Round 3 directly addresses that delay. At the specified speed, a worker starting at the entrance can travel 10 cells out, pick up a load, travel 10 back and descend 3 in `(10 + 10 + 3) / 3.5 + 0.5 = 7.1 seconds`, before recruitment or queuing. That is a credible improvement. The evidence contains no outside-player sessions on the rebuild. Below, **checked** means supported by the supplied files, including arithmetic from their values. **Assumed** marks predicted player behavior, illustrative scenarios and proposed changes. [Evidence, “Play evidence,” lines 3–13][evidence] · [02 §3.2, “Walk on a trail,” “First food” and “Porch store”][spec]

## Five causes, ranked

### 1. Blocker. The first choice taught players that their judgment was optional

**Checked.** At 15 to 30 seconds, the player can put a free husk beside the busy trail or under a stalk away from it. Ignoring the prompt sends the husk to the stalk. The script gives no benefit for choosing the stalk deliberately. Later it offers a long, open route and a *shorter* route under a sprout. On the stated dimensions of distance and exposure, both favor the same route. A particularly attentive player has little to deliberate over. The passive bot can finish with one trail, and chapter 1 ends at dusk even for a player who does nothing. [02 §4, lines 137–150, and §8.1, “Up from the deep”][spec]

**Checked.** One bird pass takes at most `5 seconds × 1 ant/second = 5 ants`, or `5 / 300 = 1.67%` of the starting adults. A single husk has nominal capacity for `3 × 3 × 4 = 36 ants`. The 12 starting pupae can all emerge by `8 + 11 × 5 = 63 seconds` on the stated schedule. Those births alone exceed the maximum ten deaths from both scripted passes. The loss counter can show five deaths while the colony total rises. The R1 test asks whether four of five people placed the husk on purpose. It never asks whether they would choose a different spot on another hill. [02 §3.2, “Colony at start,” “Bird” and “Recall,” and §4, lines 137–145][spec] · [03 R1, feature 6][features] · [04, “Milestones,” R1][roadmap]

**Assumed cause and prevention.** Players discovered they could follow the finger or wait. Both produced a growing colony and a chapter card. I would keep the bird gentle and make its effect economic. Put the quickest protein route across exposed ash. Give the sheltered approach a longer carrying time. Let a husk keep one stream of deliveries moving while the other scatters. Then reverse which route matters on a second hill. Before showing another hint, see whether a new player places cover differently and can predict which deliveries will continue. That would test judgment with the same ants, bird and drag gesture already budgeted for R1. [02 §§4, 6.2 and 7][spec] · [04, “Milestones,” Depth check][roadmap]

### 2. Blocker. The nursery and door lessons depended on events the simulation might never produce

**Checked.** The line at 3:00 says the nursery **is full**. It starts with `12 pupae + 20 larvae + 20 eggs = 52` occupants in 80 cells. A fed cohort takes `15 + 25 + 20 = 60 seconds` from egg to adult. At one egg per second, regular laying and timely feeding produce about `1 × 60 = 60` concurrent brood after the starting cohorts clear. That leaves about 20 cells available. Protein delays can alter occupancy. They do not establish a full nursery at 3:00 for a player who kept it fed. A literal builder has two incompatible instructions on an ordinary successful run. [02 §3.2, “Colony at start,” “Brood” and “Queen laying,” and §4, line 146][spec]

**Checked.** The next lesson says the third route jams on Surge. The median bot keeps crews on Steady. Door capacity is `3 cells × 6 ants/second = 18 ants/second` **in each direction**. Sending workers farther from the same door lengthens the interval between their return trips. It does not by itself raise sustained demand at the door. Multiple routes or a synchronized departure could still create a queue. The feature test sends 300 ants through one door. That establishes that a door *can* jam, while the script depends on a novice creating a meaningful jam through ordinary play. [02 §3.1, line 69, §3.2, “Doors” and “Crews,” and §4, lines 144–147][spec] · [03 R1, features 4–5][features]

**Assumed cause and prevention.** A successful player either saw a prompt to fix a problem they did not have, or saw nothing where the final two minutes needed a decision. A scripted queue would be worse: widening the entrance could appear to solve a problem that waiting would also solve. Make the lessons conditional. Offer expansion when viable brood actually needs space. Offer widening when the measured queue depresses deliveries. On a hill with spare capacity, let the player spend the same labor on a dry upper chamber before rain. Require two replays from the same opening. One player creates congestion and restores throughput by digging. Another avoids congestion through crew allocation and reaches dusk without a jam prompt. Both should have something worth doing. [02 §§3.1, 4, 6.2 and 7][spec] · [05, “Rebuild plan,” replay-lab and navigation changes][tech]

### 3. Major. The phone showed several outcomes before players could tell which one they caused

**Checked.** The opening puts 100 adults above ground and 200 below. A first delivery pops at 7 to 10 seconds, the cutaway opens for three seconds at 8 to 15 seconds, and founding pupae begin to emerge. The first beetle load reaches the nursery around 20 seconds. A genuinely player-funded adult appears around 65 seconds. The opening therefore gives the most prominent birth demonstration to a pupa that was already about to hatch. Replay instrumentation explicitly distinguishes a funded birth. The screen requirements specify counters, route rates, gap reasons and an ant inspector, but do not require a visible connection between a particular protein delivery and the resulting adult. [02 §3.1, lines 75–80, §3.2, “Colony at start,” §4, lines 133–142, and §7][spec] · [03 R1, features 1, 8 and 12][features]

**Assumed cause and prevention.** A reasonable player concluded either that seed deliveries instantly hatched ants or that births happened regardless of food. Both interpretations predict the opening spectacle. Neither gives the player a sound reason to risk the cricket route later. Preserve the founding swarm. Let players follow the first beetle load from porch to nurse to one identifiable larva. When that adult emerges, briefly show its source trail again. Keep this legible with the route card closed and sound muted. On a second attempt, give the player a hungry larva and ask what they would change. Counting a birth in the replay verifies timing. Seeing the player redirect protein verifies what the first minute taught. [02 §4, lines 135–143, §§6.1, 6.3 and 7][spec] · [04, “Risks,” starting swarm readability][roadmap]

### 4. Major. Rain punished exactly the behavior chapter 1 had rewarded

**Checked.** Chapter 1 advances at dusk and teaches the player to wait for marked placements. Chapter 2 warns at 2:00 and begins its first 30-second rain pulse at 2:30. Its ordered beats put flooding of the lowest tunnel before protein shortage and before the offer of a plug or mound. Ten seconds submerged kills brood. A structure takes 5 to 20 seconds to build. The spec says every threat first appears in a safe form, but the idle colony explicitly has no dry room for its nurses and loses when all brood drown. Marked placements or advance digging could save an attentive player. The chapter does not require the first endangered nursery to remain recoverable when the defensive offer appears. [02 §3.2, “Structure costs” and “Rain,” §6.4, “Threat order,” §6.5, and §§8.1–8.2][spec]

**Checked timing.** Using the scripted four-to-six-minute first chapter, the earliest deaths in cells flooded immediately by that pulse occur at `4:00 to 6:00 + 2:30 + 0:10 = 6:40 to 8:40` from campaign start. This is a window for first casualties. The files do not establish when the whole nursery floods. For brood already submerged, even immediate selection of a 20-second structure would finish `20 − 10 = 10 seconds` too late. Gentle mode and checkpoint retries exist. The R2a gate asks the owner to play both chapters. It does not require an unaided stranger to transfer what they learned in chapter 1 into the storm. [02 §§3.2, 4 and 8.2][spec] · [04, “Milestones,” R1 and R2a][roadmap]

**Assumed cause and prevention.** The player who patiently waited for a marker felt betrayed by the first serious loss. Show the predicted waterline during the warning. Offer a mound and an upper refuge while there is time to finish either. Let the first pulse flood an empty low passage and visibly carry water toward the nursery. Make the second pulse the first one that can destroy the brood. Test chapter 1 and chapter 2 consecutively with fresh players. Watch whether they protect the next threatened room without a second construction prompt. [02 §§6.3–6.5 and 8.1, “First rain”][spec] · [03 R2, features 15–16 and 23][features]

### 5. Major. Dusk gave players a good place to quit before it gave them a reason to return

**Checked.** The first card shows stores, ants, sprouts and losses. Chapter 2 contains the first rain. The ecological promise is stronger than either fact alone: a seed planted now can create shelter, and the nest and bloom persist across chapters. Yet the card requires only those totals and a continue button. It need not show how *this player’s* sprout or excavation changes tomorrow’s hill. The first formal test that different hills warrant different plans occurs in chapter 3, with the owner. [02 §§2, 4, 6.6 and 8.1][spec] · [03 R1, feature 11][features] · [04, “Milestones,” Depth check][roadmap]

**Checked gate, assumed example.** The recommendation question appears on the chapter card. Earlier leavers are counted separately. With 100 starters, 80 card respondents and 41 yes votes, `41 / 80 = 51.25%` clears a majority among respondents. Only `41 / 100 = 41%` of starters expressed a recommendation. The separate progression targets also permit `60% × 30% = 18%` of starters to finish Spring. Neither calculation implies the others disliked it. They show how easily a team could meet its published gates while knowing little about why people stopped. [02 §0, “What done means,” lines 11–23][spec] · [04, “Milestones,” R2c][roadmap]

**Assumed cause and prevention.** Give dusk one concrete question. Show the player’s hill at tomorrow’s predicted waterline. Their sprout protects one approach. Their lowest chamber is exposed. Let them point to the first change they intend to make, then open chapter 2 on that exact view. Keep the stop and save. Measure continuation without prompting, recommendation among respondents, and affirmative recommendations per starter. Ask early leavers what they expected their next decision to be. If they cannot name one, more chapter content will arrive too late. [02 §§2, 4, 6.5–6.6 and 8.1][spec] · [03 R1, feature 11, and R2, feature 26][features]

## Clean-room findings

**Checked, Spring.** I found no documented reuse of an authored mission, character, name, line of narration or distinctive control scheme in the release spec. The closest structural resemblance is controlling subsets of ants with pheromones. The reference binds nurseries to numbered groups and sends each group to a marker with rules of engagement. Cinderhill binds a crew to a drawn path or job. That distinction changes what the player designs, what recruits workers and what persists on the map. Likewise, painted rooms valued by depth, dryness and size differ from same-type hex adjacency upgrades. The supplied reference report paraphrases sources and identifies several details as snippet-only. This is a comparison of written designs, with no asset-level verification. ## The single assumption that sinks the project if false

**Assumed.** The project depends on strangers finding **the act of shaping a persistent colony** rewarding enough to make another plan after the initial pleasure of watching ants move. Faster movement can fix the owner’s latency complaint. Authored threats can fill quiet intervals. Neither establishes that players want to keep making decisions on this hill. [02 §§0, 2, 3.1 and 13][spec] · [04, “Risks,” depth][roadmap]

**Checked evidence and proposed test.** The owner’s 5,000-ant sandbox session lasted 1,035 seconds, with `1,035 / 81 = 12.8 seconds per order` and `2,122 × 60 / 1,035 = 123 deliveries per minute`. Their 2,000-ant sandbox session lasted 73 seconds. This is one person, and swarm size, goals and feedback differed across modes. My proposed test is two counterbalanced openings for fresh phone players: the same colony size, seed, speed and ecology, one guided through the chapter and one free to shape the hill. After the first stop, let each player leave or continue unaided. Record who continues and what change they intended to make. If neither opening produces voluntary second plans, I would stop authoring Spring. If only free play does, I would change the campaign structure while the work is still confined to one hill. [Evidence, replay table, lines 7–11][evidence] · [02 §§8.3 and 13][spec] · [03, “Lab toys,” Founding runs and Fluid colony][features]
