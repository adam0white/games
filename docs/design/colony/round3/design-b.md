# Cinderhill

You run an ant colony in a patch of ground that changes while you work. Draw routes for thousands of ants, write orders for the workers who use them, and decide which parts of the terrain to rebuild. A low trail can haul twice as much food as a ridge path until runoff fills it. You can narrow the runoff channel, span it, or move your stores uphill. Each solution changes where the next wave of workers goes and what they can carry. The first chapter gives you a working colony, a visible load of food, and a rain pulse you can prepare for in under half a minute.

| Time scale | Core loop |
|---|---|
| **30 seconds** | Draw a route, watch a load come home, see a new worker join, and commit your first construction bundle before the rain reaches the trail. |
| **10 minutes** | Finish a chapter in five to seven minutes. Spend the rest improving the same colony’s route plan or starting the next site. A complete chapter has an opening, a disruption, and a recovery you can see on the map. |
| **5 hours** | Revisit terrain with different weather, build larger colonies, and improve throughput under tighter constraints. Take a difficult site into a shared colony or stay in free play with 5,000 ants and reshape the map at your own pace. |

## The first five minutes

The chapter is **Rain at the Sill**. One screen shows the nest mouth, a seed cluster 13 cells away, and two ways across a shallow drainage line. Near the mouth, 24 workers mill around an active sugar route. A small brood panel shows one pupa due to emerge. The nest holds 20 sugar and one bundle of construction fiber. A high patch beside the source is marked as a future cache. The opening objective is **Stock the high cache before the second rain**.

The recommended construction sites are a **span** across the low drainage line and a **ramp** up to the cache. The span keeps the short harvest route open at limited capacity during the first rain. The ramp puts loads into high storage sooner while the low route closes briefly. Both sites preview where ants will walk, the loads per minute under the forecast, and where workers will wait if capacity is exceeded. The first bundle buys either structure. The colony can earn enough fiber for the other later.

This is the reference replay at normal speed. The clock begins when the map accepts input. The player sends the first order at **2 seconds**, gets the first delivery at **9**, sees the first birth at **13**, makes the first consequential choice at **17**, and encounters the first threat at **24**. These are targets for the reference route. If the player hesitates, the forecast remains visible and the high ground provides a recovery route. The game clock stops while solo tactical hold is open.

From **1:00 to 2:00**, the player stocks the cache, sees the forecast for a longer pulse, and chooses whether to dig a second shaft onto the ridge. The starting queen chamber has room for one additional excavation before it needs reinforcement. A shaft shortens future trips. A store chamber can hold food close to the brood and frees surface workers to haul to the cache. The player can finish with either chamber and can place the other after reinforcement.

From **2:00 to 3:30**, the second rain cuts the low ground for longer. Sand carried by the water partly fills the shallow channel and changes the water’s exit. The forecast shows the new route before the pulse. Workers can deepen the channel, take the ridge, or stage loads beside the span. A player who missed the first construction window can use the ridge and open the cache with recovered fiber. The chapter allows a first-time player to recover while protecting the cache objective.

From **3:30 to 5:00**, the immediate task is to bring the cache to **18 protein**, then supply **six loads to the nursery during or after the second pulse**. Construction and brood spending draw from the same transport network. When both conditions hold and the water recedes, a short map summary shows how many loads crossed each route and where ants waited. The player can continue managing the colony, start the next chapter, or replay the same site with the forecast visible from the beginning. The reference replay finishes around **4:45**. A slower player can finish after five minutes while the colony remains viable.

## Systems and decisions

### Control the work

The player commands routes and jobs. Ants choose trips within those orders. Drag from a nest entrance, cache, or junction to a resource or work site. The route keeps its drawn shape wherever the terrain permits. An impassable segment stays visibly broken until the player moves it or previews a local detour. A route card exposes three decisions in place: **worker allocation**, **drop-off**, and **priority**. Each is editable while the game runs. A route defaults to taking available workers up to the source’s useful capacity. The player can change the cap when two routes compete.

Use a one-finger drag to draw from a highlighted endpoint and to pan from empty ground. Tap a route to edit it. Pinch to zoom. Keep a tap-to-place path mode for players who find drawing difficult. Surface and nest buttons retain the selected job and camera anchor across layers. Desktop uses the same gestures with a mouse, with keys for tactical hold, layer switching, and cycling active routes. Live overlays use arrows, texture, and line weight as well as color. Route stats show queue location and direction so the player can trace a missed delivery to a specific crossing or door.

The decision is **where to send the next available worker**. A crew can lift more food from a rich patch, build a bypass before rain, or reinforce a passage under pressure. A full queue makes another worker worth more elsewhere. Moving capacity also has a cost. Loaded ants finish their trips and unloaded ants can reassign at the next junction.

### Feed, build, and grow

**Sugar** funds worker activity and excavation. **Protein** funds brood and chapter objectives. **Fiber** pays for surface structures. Each resource has a distinct pickup silhouette and can be staged at a cache. Sugar demand comes mainly from assigned work. When supply falls, priority jobs keep their reserve and low-priority jobs slow. A small colony can scavenge nearby enough to restart basic hauling. A depleted source can close a route and free its workers automatically. The player sees the projected shortfall before either event.

Approve brood in batches of **four workers** or **two majors**. The opening full-term pupa and nearly mature starting larva produce the chapter’s fast births. Later batches depend on food, available nursery space, and nurses. Aim for **45–90 seconds** from funding to adulthood in a supplied colony. A batch commits its protein at funding. A blocked nursery shows the limiting condition and holds that commitment until space frees or the player cancels.

The decision is **whether to turn present surplus into throughput or defense**. Workers can build, haul, and support a junction. Majors hold narrow ground while workers pass behind them. A major crew consumes protein and sugar and occupies passage capacity. Nest placement changes the same arithmetic. A store near the surface relieves the entry queue, while a store deep in the nest can shorten brood supply. A second shaft reduces travel while creating a second point to defend from rain or intruders.

### Fight for access

Combat centers on junctions, bypasses, and retreat paths. Orders at a junction are **hold**, **screen**, and **withdraw**. Hold keeps majors stationed there. Screen protects passing carriers and returns them to their route as the threat passes. Withdraw clears the junction and frees the crew. Opposing colonies contest routes and pick up dropped cargo. Combat shows ants scattering, retreating, or disappearing under cover. A defeated crew reduces available workers. The route remains on the map so the player can reroute or reclaim it.

Threats announce their direction and behavior before contact. A rival scout follows traffic toward a valuable junction. A foraging bird shadow closes exposed lanes for seconds at a time while covered paths keep moving. Water closes cells according to the terrain and can change the map at the end of a pulse. The decision is **which ground matters enough to hold**. Giving up an entrance may save a crew at the cost of isolating a store. Feeding a fight can win the junction while losing the time needed to complete the chapter objective.

### Change the map and move on

Use a top-down surface and a linked nest cutaway. Surface affordances include low channels, high ground, shaded cover, and anchor points. Underground affordances include soft soil, packed soil, stable walls, and vulnerable shafts. Excavation follows connected grid cells. The dug shape determines capacity and connectivity. Surface structures occupy sockets placed along legal edges. Where the forecast depends on the player’s plan, show the predicted change before committing fiber or excavation labor.

**Spans preserve a connection. Ramps change who can reach an elevation. Channels change where water goes.** Every placement has an opportunity cost and a downstream consequence. A stronger channel can protect the nursery shaft while directing water toward a useful seed bed. Opening a lower outlet can expose a food patch when the rain recedes. Advanced chapters combine two forces early enough for the player to act on both.

Campaign progress unlocks maps and additional project types. It carries forward the player’s cleared chapters, replay records, and preferences. Each chapter starts with a curated colony and stockpile, so an expensive victory leaves the next site playable. Within a site, improvements remain when the player continues in free play. Optional challenges ask for different outcomes on the same map, such as keeping two entrances open, finishing with a small workforce, or supplying a remote brood room through successive rain pulses. Show those challenges after the first clear. The player decides **which constraint to take on next**.

## Pace, sessions, and return play

A chapter lasts **five to eight minutes** for a first clear. A normal session is **eight to fifteen minutes**, including one or two chapters or one extended free-play visit. Save solo at an order boundary and preserve the sim tick on resume. A chapter ends when its objective and hazard window resolve, or when flooding or rivals sever all viable routes to the queen and the player has no recovery order available. Show the consequence, then offer a chapter restart or one return to the last chapter checkpoint.

Free play is available from the first menu. Give it starting sizes of **200, 1,000, and 5,000**, an immediate live trail, and free placement of sources and weather. The owner spent 17 minutes issuing 81 orders in the 5,000-ant sandbox. That session supports keeping direct swarm control available from the start. The campaign earns its place by supplying decisions about the swarm’s work.

A return visit opens at the last save or the next chapter. A shared weekly seed gives cleared sites a fresh layout and forecast. Each seed remains playable after its week. Compare replay outcomes by loads delivered, objectives met, and colony losses. Skip streaks and expiring rewards.

| Replay measure | Acceptance target |
|---|---|
| Chapter 1 scripted opening | First order committed by 4 s, first delivery by 12 s, first adult by 16 s, first construction commitment by 20 s, first hazard by 28 s |
| Both initial builds | Span-first and ramp-first runs both clear. At least one route or cache delivery milestone differs before the first pulse ends. |
| Delayed or broken opening | With no seed trail for the first 30 s, a scripted recovery can still clear. An unreachable trail displays the blocked segment on release. |
| Decision frequency | At least four distinct orders in the first two minutes change a measured state, such as stock, queue, access, or brood. From 0:30 to 4:00, each 45 s window has a new actionable condition or a material change to a prior order. |
| Chapter 1 completion | Reference replay clears between 4:30 and 5:30. A recovery replay clears by 8:00. |
| Unassisted play | At least 8 of 10 first-time players issue a useful order in 10 s, and at least 8 clear within 8 min. After the clear, ask whether they would recommend it and why. Test on real phones and desktop. |

Log tick-stamped order acceptance, delivery, birth, threat onset, topology changes, and clear conditions. Use bot checks for timing and solvability. Use observed play for whether the choices made sense and whether players wanted another chapter.

## Friends by link

Two to four friends run **one colony** on a co-op expedition. The host picks a site and shares a private link. Players choose a display name and can join immediately. All players can act on the surface and in the nest. On a four-player map, two can manage separate foraging fronts while a third builds a route between them and a fourth supplies the brood and manages reserves. These are jobs the map creates. Players can swap them mid-run. Pings mark a site, request workers, or propose a route.

Each route or project has an editor shown by name. An editor can hand off a job. The room can reclaim a job after a disconnect window. The host can clear abandoned claims. A reservation for fiber shows who has committed it and to which site. The server checks the reservation and the resource debit in the same ordered command. A conflicting order returns a reason and a refreshed preview. When players join or rejoin, they see the current forecast, shared objective, and active projects before taking control. Co-op runs continuously while someone remains connected. When everyone leaves, the room checkpoints and resumes from that tick on return. Later, add a two-colony contest for a common transport objective with separate queens and catchments.

Reuse the deterministic 20 Hz integer simulation. The measurements in the brief establish cross-runtime hash agreement and a roughly 60 KB late-join snapshot with 5,000 ants. Treat those as baselines. In solo, run the same sim in a Web Worker and keep saves and replays in browser storage. In co-op, use a Worker to resolve invite links and a Durable Object as the authority for room ticks, identity tokens, command order, and snapshots. Clients render local sim replicas and interpolate motion between ticks. A hash mismatch requests a fresh snapshot and command suffix. Checkpoint room state and acknowledge recovery from the last committed tick after a server restart. Keep the sim independent of rendering and network arrival time. Profile active rooms with the final rules on workerd and profile rendering and input on physical phones.

## Three ideas worth the game

1. **The player changes the route network itself.** A drawing becomes a transport route, and terrain work alters that route’s capacity and future availability. It matters which side of a channel a cache sits on. The map after rain can reward a project whose first use was modest. This makes construction, weather, and economy parts of the same tactical decision.
2. **Traffic is the resource the player can redirect in seconds.** The visible swarm lets the player find a queue and act on it. Opening a bypass, prioritizing loaded returns, or asking majors to screen a choke can change how much the colony gets done before the next pulse. A large colony makes an existing problem more interesting because two busy fronts compete for the same crossings and reserves.
3. **Chapter goals and free play use the same colony rules.** A chapter asks the player to solve a specific problem with a time and place. The player can stay on that map, inherit every project they built, and try another plan with a bigger swarm. SimAnt’s surface and underground layers are a genre precedent. Cinderhill’s distinct unit of play is the engineered connection between them and the load it can carry when the map changes.

## Build order

1. **Deliver the opening on both inputs.** Keep the existing sim contracts, route drawing, replay hashes, renderer, and linked views. Build the complete Rain at the Sill route, both project choices, and the recoverable opening. Play it on real phones before expanding the campaign.
2. **Make the systems interact.** Add cache endpoints, construction, excavation, nurse and brood allocation, weather-driven topology, and junction combat. Build a small set of maps that force different choices. Test delivery and terrain forecasts against actual replay results.
3. **Complete the solo release.** Ship eight authored chapters, checkpoint and resume, optional challenge conditions, free play up to 5,000 ants, procedural Field and Ember presentation, and accessible route controls. Use the first chapter as the onboarding sequence. Chapters three through eight should each require a different use of the existing systems.
4. **Complete link co-op and release as a whole game.** Ship three tuned expedition maps for two to four friends, reconnect, shared replay, and explicit room ownership of orders. Verify the published build through a fresh browser and a link opened on another physical phone.
5. **Extend mastery.** Add weekly seeds, the two-colony contest, and maps with dynamic outlets and additional underground interactions. Use replay evidence and player sessions to choose which projects deserve another chapter.

If scope tightens, cut the two-colony contest, the weekly feed, advanced soil interactions, and extra expedition maps in that order. Preserve the full opening, authored solo progression, free play, and one polished co-op map. Use a shared code-art pipeline. Field gets readable silhouettes, baked-looking stepped tones, and patterned trails. Ember uses the same silhouettes with warm edge lighting and an optional reduced-effects setting.

## Five risks and early tests

| Risk | Early test |
|---|---|
| **The opening is busy but still feels slow.** | Give first-time players the complete first chapter with no spoken guidance. Watch the moment after the first delivery. Ask players to explain what they changed and why. If they wait for the next cue or treat the build sockets as equivalent, revise the visible consequences before adding content. |
| **Touch edits order the wrong job.** | Test crossing trails, small sockets, one-handed panning, cancel, and pinch during drawing on physical phones. Have players move a drop-off while a threat is active. Measure unintended orders and whether the next input corrects them. |
| **One construction or caste strategy dominates.** | Run span-first, ramp-first, excavation-first, and minimal-building policies across authored seeds. Compare stock, recovery time, and losses. Hand two players the same replay seed and ask each to find a better plan for a different objective. Tune maps where the same opening wins all objectives. |
| **Final rules exceed the existing sim or renderer budget.** | Stress 5,000 ants with rain, excavation, combat, and simultaneous route edits. Measure tick percentiles, render stalls, input-to-feedback, and sustained performance on physical phones. Target sim p95 at or below 15 ms against a 50 ms tick budget, and a sustained 30 fps on the phone floor selected for release. Verify hashes after a 60-minute soak. |
| **Co-op loses commands or makes friends fight the UI.** | Have four people change shared projects, reserve the last fiber together, and rejoin over a throttled mobile connection. Restart the Durable Object between committed commands. Check for duplicate charges, lost acknowledged orders, hash divergence, and uncertainty over who controls each project. |

The timing, economy, and performance thresholds above are design targets. The supplied measurements cover the current sim and emulated rendering. Physical-phone performance, independent first-chapter completion, and willingness to recommend remain to be measured.

## Owner choices

1. **How should Field and Ember appear in the campaign?**  
   **Field campaign** uses daylight throughout and offers Ember in free play. **Alternating chapters** gives each site a fixed light treatment. **Time of day** uses both in one site with a gradual transition and the same tactical overlays.

2. **What tone should explain the colony’s work?**  
   **Natural history** uses short habitat notes and observed behavior. **Colony ledger** uses objective cards, route records, and weather marks. **Wordless scenes** uses small animated moments at chapter boundaries and keeps written text in controls and objectives.

3. **What should a close win feel like?**  
   **Rescue the work** emphasizes clearing a route and getting the last loads through. **Hold the ground** emphasizes keeping two fronts supplied under pressure. **Outbuild the forecast** emphasizes finishing a terrain change early enough to open an entirely different route.
